The last ten minutes of the day decide whether you actually know how your cafe is doing. A proper end-of-day close — counting the drawer, matching every rupee of cash and digital payment against sales, and resolving any gap — is the single most protective habit an owner can build. Skip it and small leaks go unnoticed for months. Do it every night and problems surface while they are still small enough to fix.

## Why the close protects you

When you are not on the floor every hour, the daily close is how you keep watch without hovering. It does three things at once:

- **It catches leakage early.** A drawer that comes up short two nights in a row is a signal you can act on now, not a mystery you discover in a quarterly panic.
- **It keeps your numbers honest.** Your recorded sales, your cash, and your digital receipts should all tell the same story. When they do, your books are trustworthy — for you and for the [IRD](/blog/vat-ird-billing-for-restaurants).
- **It builds a culture of accountability.** When staff know the drawer is counted every night, casual "borrowing" and sloppy change-making mostly stop on their own.

> A discrepancy is not an accusation. It is information. The point of the close is to surface it while you can still explain it.

## The close, step by step

Run the same sequence every night. Consistency is what makes the numbers comparable from day to day.

### 1. Stop and cut off

Settle any open tables and stop taking new orders on the till you are closing. You cannot reconcile a drawer that is still moving. If you run late tables, close the shift and start a fresh count for the after-hours service.

### 2. Pull your sales total by payment method

Before you touch the cash, get your sales figure broken down: how much was cash, how much eSewa, how much Khalti, how much card or bank transfer. This is the target your counts must hit. If you tagged payment method on every bill during service — as you should — this is a single report. A POS like GoServe produces this split at the tap of a button, which is what makes the rest of the close fast.

### 3. Count the drawer

Count the physical cash carefully.

- Count notes and coins and write down the total.
- **Subtract the opening float** (the cash you started the day with — say Rs 3,000). What remains is your cash sales for the day.
- **Add back any cash paid out of the drawer** during the day — a supplier paid in cash, petty expenses — because that money left as expense, not as missing cash. Keep the chits.

Your adjusted cash figure should match the cash sales from your sales report.

### 4. Match the digital payments

For eSewa and Khalti, confirm the received amounts against your own account records, not against customer screenshots. Add up what actually landed and compare to the eSewa and Khalti totals on your sales report. Do the same for card and bank transfers. Each method should reconcile on its own — this is why tagging the method per bill matters so much.

### 5. Note the result

Write down the outcome for each method: matched, short, or over, and by how much. A close that is exactly zero every single night is nice, but small variances happen. What matters is that you record them and watch the pattern.

## Handling discrepancies

When something does not match, resolve it calmly and methodically.

1. **Recount first.** Most discrepancies are counting errors or the float being added or subtracted wrong.
2. **Check for untagged or miscategorised payments.** A cash sale accidentally marked as eSewa will make one short and the other over by the same amount — a classic offsetting pair that points straight at the fix.
3. **Look for unlogged payouts and comps.** Cash paid to a supplier without a chit, or a free drink never recorded, both look like a shortage.
4. **Check your digital confirmations.** A payment the customer "sent" that never actually arrived is a real loss, not a counting error — and worth catching the same night.
5. **Log it either way.** If you cannot explain a small gap, still record it. A patterns of Rs 50 shortages tells you more over a month than any single night does.

Set a threshold for what counts as normal. A few rupees of rounding is noise. A consistent shortage, or one large gap, deserves a real conversation.

## Make it a fixed routine

The close only protects you if it happens the same way every night, even — especially — when it was busy.

- **Same person, same time, same order of steps.** Assign who closes so it is never "someone will do it".
- **Keep the opening float constant.** A fixed float (and counting it at open) is what makes the close subtractable. A random starting amount makes every close guesswork.
- **Keep the chits.** Every cash payout needs a slip. No slip, no deduction — it just reads as missing money.
- **Do it before the fatigue.** Close while you can still think, not at 1am when every number blurs.

This close mirrors the [opening routine](/blog/running-a-cafe-in-nepal) — a known float at open, a matched count at close. The two bookend your day and keep the cash story unbroken.

## The payoff

A cafe that closes its books every night runs with a quiet confidence a cafe that does not simply cannot have. You know your real daily sales. You know cash and digital match. You catch leaks in days, not quarters. And when tax time comes, your records already agree with your takings, so there is nothing to reconstruct or explain.

Ten minutes a night is a small price for knowing exactly where your money is. Build the habit, keep it consistent, and let the numbers do the watching for you.
